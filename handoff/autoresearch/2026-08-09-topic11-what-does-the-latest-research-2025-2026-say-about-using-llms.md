# Large Language Models as Alpha Signals from Corporate Communication: Management Guidance, Earnings Call Tone, and Disclosure Evasion

## Introduction

The application of large language models (LLMs) to unstructured financial text — earnings call transcripts, management guidance, and analyst Q&A sessions — has moved from a niche natural language processing exercise to a central question in quantitative finance: can linguistic signals extracted by LLMs generate tradable, statistically robust alpha, or do they merely reproduce classification accuracy that fails to survive contact with real markets? Research published between late 2025 and mid-2026 increasingly separates these two questions explicitly, and the emerging picture is considerably more cautious than the marketing language surrounding commercial "earnings sentiment" products would suggest ([Earnings Whispers, n.d.](https://www.earningswhispers.com/)). This report synthesizes the most recent and methodologically substantive evidence available — a peer-reviewed-style benchmark study, a bias-auditing position paper, an ICLR 2026 workshop project on disclosure-metric drift, and a partially legible academic conference paper — to assess what is actually known about scoring management tone, guidance, and evasive communication as an alpha signal, with particular attention to reproducibility and look-ahead-bias safeguards.

## The Central Empirical Finding: Classification Accuracy Does Not Imply Return Predictability

The single most rigorous and directly relevant piece of evidence is a 2026 benchmark study that explicitly tests the gap between linguistic sentiment-classification quality and downstream economic validity ([Luo, 2026](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.04200)). The study first builds a unified three-class sentiment benchmark from five financial text datasets and compares TF–IDF Naive Bayes, off-the-shelf FinBERT and Financial-RoBERTa encoders, zero-shot Qwen2.5-7B, and QLoRA-fine-tuned Qwen2.5-7B, LLaMA3-8B, and Mistral-7B models ([Luo, 2026](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.04200)).

| Model | Test Accuracy | Macro-F1 |
|---|---|---|
| Mistral-7B (QLoRA) | 0.8840 (best) | 0.8771 |
| Qwen2.5-7B (QLoRA) | — | 0.8615 (up from 0.7274 zero-shot) |
| LLaMA3-8B (QLoRA) | — | competitive but not top |
| Zero-shot Qwen2.5-7B | — | 0.7274 baseline |

*Source: [Luo (2026)](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.04200).*

These results confirm that parameter-efficient fine-tuning (QLoRA) meaningfully improves financial sentiment classification. However, the study's second and more consequential experiment tests economic validity on a **temporally separate** 2019 Benzinga sample of 10,637 unique headlines and 13,115 headline–stock observations across a fixed S&P 100 universe — a design choice that directly guards against look-ahead bias by ensuring the evaluation period does not overlap with model training or tuning data ([Luo, 2026](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.04200)). Model probabilities were converted into continuous sentiment scores, aggregated by stock and signal date, and aligned with next-session returns at one-, two-, three-, and five-day horizons.

The result: all seven models produced only small, positive mean rank information coefficients at the one-day horizon, with the largest — 0.0143 for FinBERT — falling well below thresholds generally considered economically meaningful ([Luo, 2026](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.04200)). Critically, after applying Newey–West heteroskedasticity- and autocorrelation-consistent inference and false-discovery-rate correction across the 28 model–horizon combinations tested, **none remained statistically significant**, and portfolio-level backtests likewise failed to establish a robust performance advantage for the best-performing classifiers ([Luo, 2026](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.04200)). This is a methodologically important result precisely because it is reproducible in the sense that matters most: it explicitly separates in-sample linguistic performance from out-of-sample, multiple-testing-corrected economic performance, rather than reporting an uncorrected headline correlation.

## Management Guidance and the "Moving Targets" Phenomenon

A conceptually distinct but complementary line of research targets not sentiment polarity but *behavioral change* in what management chooses to emphasize. Work accepted at the ICLR 2026 Advances in Financial AI Workshop — described publicly as "From Text to Alpha: Can LLMs Track Evolving Signals in Corporate Disclosures?" — documents that management teams quietly substitute the performance metrics they highlight on earnings calls when growth stalls, for example dropping "revenue growth" in favor of "cost savings" ([Choi, 2026](https://www.linkedin.com/posts/chanyeolchoi_from-text-to-alpha-can-llms-track-evolving-activity-7439210992536014848-guxG)). This "moving targets" behavior is characterized in the source as having been previously shown to predict lower future returns, framing metric substitution itself as an alpha-relevant signal distinct from raw tone ([Choi, 2026](https://www.linkedin.com/posts/chanyeolchoi_from-text-to-alpha-can-llms-track-evolving-activity-7439210992536014848-guxG)).

The proposed detection method, "LLM as Extractor, Embedding as Ruler," uses an LLM to read transcripts and extract context-aware performance metrics while preserving qualifiers that materially change meaning (e.g., "North America" revenue growth versus global revenue growth), and then uses embedding-based comparison to measure drift or substitution in the metrics emphasized quarter over quarter ([Choi, 2026](https://www.linkedin.com/posts/chanyeolchoi_from-text-to-alpha-can-llms-track-evolving-activity-7439210992536014848-guxG)). The post explicitly argues that traditional named-entity-recognition approaches fail here because they extract only surface patterns ("the %," "a % increase") and miss the substantive business metric buried in context — a claim consistent with why LLM-based extraction is being proposed as an improvement over legacy NLP pipelines ([Choi, 2026](https://www.linkedin.com/posts/chanyeolchoi_from-text-to-alpha-can-llms-track-evolving-activity-7439210992536014848-guxG)).

This is the strand of research most directly relevant to "management guidance" as an alpha construct, but it is important to be transparent about an evidentiary limitation: the available material is a secondary LinkedIn summary of a workshop-accepted project, not the primary paper, and it does not disclose quantitative backtest statistics (information coefficients, Sharpe ratios, or significance tests) comparable to those reported by Luo (2026). Acceptance at an ICLR workshop lends some credibility, but workshop tracks typically apply lighter review than main-conference tracks, and no reproducibility artifact (code, data, or out-of-sample test) is described in the sourced content.

## Reproducibility and Look-Ahead Bias: A Field-Wide Diagnosis

The strongest and most actionable evidence on the reproducibility question comes from a 2026 position paper that reviewed 164 financial-LLM papers published between 2023 and 2025 and identified five recurring, compounding sources of invalid results: look-ahead bias, survivorship bias, narrative bias, objective bias, and cost bias ([Kong et al., 2026](https://arxiv.org/html/2602.14233v1)). The paper's central quantitative finding is stark: **no single bias category was even discussed in more than 28% of the reviewed studies**, meaning the large majority of financial LLM papers do not address, let alone control for, these validity threats ([Kong et al., 2026](https://arxiv.org/html/2602.14233v1)). The authors argue that these biases "often compound to create an illusion of validity" and propose a Structural Validity Framework with a minimal evaluation checklist, alongside a public repository of bias-mitigation resources ([Kong et al., 2026](https://arxiv.org/html/2602.14233v1)).

| Bias type | Description (per Kong et al., 2026) | Relevance to guidance/tone/evasion signals |
|---|---|---|
| Look-ahead bias | Model or evaluation exposed to information not available at signal time | Directly threatens any backtest of earnings-call sentiment unless training/test periods are temporally separated |
| Survivorship bias | Universe excludes delisted/failed firms | Affects fixed-universe studies (e.g., S&P 100/500) that omit companies that exited the index |
| Narrative bias | Selective storytelling around favorable results | Relevant to both guidance-tone studies and the papers reporting them |
| Objective bias | Misalignment between evaluation metric and real trading objective | Explains why high classification accuracy (Luo, 2026) does not translate into portfolio alpha |
| Cost bias | Ignoring transaction costs, latency, and implementation frictions | Rarely addressed in the sourced studies' return-predictability tests |

Measured against this checklist, [Luo (2026)](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.04200) stands out as unusually rigorous: it uses a temporally separate holdout sample (mitigating look-ahead bias), a fixed but stated stock universe, and formal multiple-testing correction (mitigating a specific form of narrative/objective bias in reporting). By contrast, the ICLR workshop project on moving targets ([Choi, 2026](https://www.linkedin.com/posts/chanyeolchoi_from-text-to-alpha-can-llms-track-evolving-activity-7439210992536014848-guxG)) and the AEA conference paper ([American Economic Association, 2026](https://www.aeaweb.org/conference/2026/program/paper/zNrQ4Yn6)) do not disclose enough methodological detail in the available sources to be assessed against the same checklist, which is itself consistent with Kong et al.'s (2026) finding that such disclosure is the exception rather than the norm in this literature.

## Assessment and Concrete Conclusions

Based on the evidence assembled here, three conclusions can be stated with reasonable confidence rather than hedged generically:

1. **Sentiment-polarity signals from earnings-related text, even when generated by fine-tuned open-weight LLMs achieving strong classification accuracy (up to 0.884), do not currently constitute a statistically robust or tradable alpha source once temporal separation and multiple-testing correction are properly applied.** The Luo (2026) result — 0 of 28 model–horizon tests surviving Newey–West/FDR correction — is the most methodologically complete test in the sourced evidence, and it should be weighted heavily against any claim of validated LLM-tone alpha ([Luo, 2026](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.04200)).

2. **Structural, behavior-based signals — such as detecting when management substitutes the metrics it emphasizes ("moving targets") — are conceptually more promising than raw sentiment scoring because they target a documented incentive to obscure deteriorating performance, which is closer to genuine "evasion."** However, this claim currently rests on workshop-stage, non-quantitatively-disclosed evidence, and should be treated as a promising research direction rather than a validated signal until a full paper with out-of-sample backtests is available ([Choi, 2026](https://www.linkedin.com/posts/chanyeolchoi_from-text-to-alpha-can-llms-track-evolving-activity-7439210992536014848-guxG)).

3. **No source reviewed here contains a study that isolates "Q&A evasion" specifically (e.g., evasive or non-responsive answers during the analyst question period) as a standalone, quantitatively tested alpha factor.** This is a genuine gap in the available evidence rather than a finding that such signals do not work — it simply means the claim cannot be supported or refuted from the material at hand, and it should not be conflated with the tone/sentiment or moving-targets findings above, which measure different constructs.

Given that Kong et al. (2026) find fewer than 28% of 164 recent financial-LLM papers even discuss look-ahead bias, the practical implication for any practitioner evaluating a vendor or research claim in this space is to demand explicit disclosure of (a) temporal separation between model development and test period, (b) a survivorship-bias-free or explicitly stated universe, (c) transaction-cost-adjusted returns, and (d) correction for multiple comparisons — the same checklist that distinguishes Luo (2026) from the other sources reviewed here ([Kong et al., 2026](https://arxiv.org/html/2602.14233v1); [Luo, 2026](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.04200)). Commercial platforms that market "earnings sentiment" scoring without disclosing this methodology, such as the sentiment-visualization tools referenced on Earnings Whispers, should be treated as informational products rather than validated alpha sources absent independent verification ([Earnings Whispers, n.d.](https://www.earningswhispers.com/)).

## Conclusion

As of August 2026, the weight of the most methodologically transparent evidence indicates that LLM-derived scores of earnings-call tone and headline sentiment do not survive rigorous, bias-aware testing as standalone alpha signals, even though the underlying classification models have become highly accurate. The more promising frontier lies in structural extraction of managerial behavior — such as metric substitution under the "moving targets" framework — but this evidence remains early-stage and quantitatively undisclosed in public sources. Meanwhile, a documented, field-wide failure to check for look-ahead, survivorship, and objective bias across the majority of published financial-LLM research means that any new claim of LLM-based earnings alpha, including on management guidance or Q&A tone, should be treated with default skepticism until it demonstrates the same level of methodological disclosure found in the strongest study reviewed here.
