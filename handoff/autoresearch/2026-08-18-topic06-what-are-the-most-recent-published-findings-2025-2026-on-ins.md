# Insider Trading Signals and LLM Classification: Evaluating Form 4 Filings as Alpha Sources (2025–2026)

## Introduction

SEC Form 4 filings — the disclosures corporate insiders must submit within two business days of buying or selling shares in their own company — have been studied as a source of tradable alpha for roughly sixty years ([Johnsen & Shasharina, 2026](https://tommijohnsen.substack.com/p/most-of-the-insider-trading-alpha)). What has changed in the 2025–2026 period is not the existence of the signal but two parallel developments: (1) a renewed academic effort to decompose *where* insider-trading alpha actually resides in the transaction-to-disclosure timeline, and (2) the emergence of large language model (LLM) benchmarking work aimed at automating classification and extraction from SEC filings more broadly. This report synthesizes the most recent, credible findings on both fronts, evaluates whether the two literatures have actually converged into a combined "Form 4 + LLM classification" hit-rate discipline, and offers a concrete assessment of what the evidence supports going into late 2026.

## The Legacy Baseline Against Which 2025–2026 Research Is Measured

Before evaluating what is new, it is useful to establish the consensus baseline that recent studies are reacting to. Industry summaries built on decades of academic replication place the excess return generated by following insider purchases at approximately 4–8% annually above market benchmarks, with the strongest signals coming from cluster buying (three or more insiders purchasing within a short window), large dollar-value purchases, and C-suite transactions at smaller-capitalization companies ([TradeAlgo, n.d.](https://www.tradealgo.com/trading-guides/stocks/insider-trading-signals-how-to-track-sec-form-4-filings-for-investment-ideas)). This same source notes an important asymmetry that recurs throughout the literature: insider *sales* carry little informational value because they are frequently driven by diversification, tax planning, or liquidity needs, whereas insider *purchases* are more informative because they are voluntary and capital-committing. Rule 10b5-1 pre-scheduled trading plans are explicitly flagged as lower-signal because the trade decision predates the information environment at execution time ([TradeAlgo, n.d.](https://www.tradealgo.com/trading-guides/stocks/insider-trading-signals-how-to-track-sec-form-4-filings-for-investment-ideas)). This baseline — positive but modest, sales-filtered, purchase-weighted, cluster-sensitive alpha realized over a 6–12 month holding horizon rather than an intraday one — is the yardstick the newer 2025–2026 empirical work is measured against, and as the next section shows, that newer work is considerably less optimistic about how much of this alpha survives to the point of public disclosure.

## 2025–2026 Academic Findings on Insider-Trading Alpha

### Does Speed Still Pay? Evidence from Oenschläger and Möllenhoff (2025)

The most directly relevant peer-reviewed contribution identified in the available sources is Oenschläger and Möllenhoff's 2025 study in *Finance Research Letters*, titled "Insider filings as trading signals — Does it pay to be fast?" Using intraday data in the post–Sarbanes-Oxley period, the authors tested whether a prompt, mechanical reaction to Form 4 filing announcements would generate abnormal returns ([Oenschläger & Möllenhoff, 2025](https://ideas.repec.org/a/eee/finlet/v72y2025ics1544612324015435.html)). Their findings are meaningfully more conservative than the historical 4–8% consensus:

| Finding | Detail |
|---|---|
| Direction of abnormal return | Positive, but lower than prior studies for short holding periods |
| Effect of position sizing | Returns vanish and even turn negative once tradable dollar amounts are capped at realistic (non-scalable) levels |
| Liquidity relationship | Returns are negatively correlated with stock liquidity |
| Scalability | The strategy is "almost negated" as a profitable, scalable approach even before transaction costs are considered |

*Table 1. Summary of Oenschläger and Möllenhoff (2025) findings on speed-based Form 4 trading strategies.*

This is a significant recalibration: it suggests that reacting quickly to the *public* filing itself — the step an automated or LLM-driven pipeline would target — is not where the historically cited alpha comes from. The signal that remains after realistic position sizing and liquidity constraints is thin, and it degrades further for the small-cap, low-float names where insider signals are traditionally considered strongest, precisely because those are the least liquid.

### Where Does the Alpha Actually Live? The Ozlen and Batumoglu Decomposition (2026)

A complementary and more direct explanation for why fast-reaction strategies underperform comes from a January 2026 SSRN paper by Ozlen and Batumoglu, discussed in a May 2026 analysis by Johnsen and Shasharina. The paper decomposes total insider-trading alpha into two components: the portion realized between the actual transaction date and the public filing date (an unobservable window to outside investors), versus the portion accessible after the filing becomes public ([Johnsen & Shasharina, 2026](https://tommijohnsen.substack.com/p/most-of-the-insider-trading-alpha)). Their central finding is stark: **70–80% of the apparent historical return lives in the unobservable pre-filing window**, not in the publicly tradable post-filing period. Johnsen and Shasharina draw the direct implication for production trading systems: "most of the alpha is captured by people closer to the transaction than a daily-cadence pipeline can be. Run a strategy on filing-date Form 4 data, act at the next open, and you're working against a strong headwind that the historical academic backtests don't account for" ([Johnsen & Shasharina, 2026](https://tommijohnsen.substack.com/p/most-of-the-insider-trading-alpha)).

Read together, the Oenschläger and Möllenhoff (2025) and Ozlen and Batumoglu (2026) findings tell a coherent and, in my assessment, fairly conclusive story: the academic literature has moved from asking "is there alpha in Form 4 filings?" (yes, historically) to asking "is any of that alpha still capturable by someone reading the filing after it is public?" — and the answer emerging in 2025–2026 is that very little is. This matters directly for the LLM-classification question, because faster parsing of a public filing cannot recover alpha that, by these papers' own accounting, was already realized before the filing existed.

## The Emerging LLM-Classification Literature on SEC Filings

### Benchmarking LLMs on Filing-Level Tasks: Fin-RATE and the AIMultiple Survey

Separately from the insider-trading alpha literature, a distinct 2025–2026 research thread has focused on how well LLMs classify and extract information from SEC filings generally, without a specific focus on Form 4 or insider-trading alpha. A 2026 benchmark called Fin-RATE — described as "a real-world financial analytics and tracking evaluation benchmark for LLMs on SEC filings" — is cited as one of the more recent efforts to formally evaluate model performance on filing-derived tasks ([IntuitionLabs.ai, 2026](https://intuitionlabs.ai/articles/llm-financial-document-analysis)). This sits alongside a 2025 AIMultiple benchmark comparing 38 LLMs on finance-oriented tasks, and commercial tooling such as AlphaSense's Deep Research product and Brightwave, which raised a $15 million Series A in 2025 specifically to build LLM-based financial research infrastructure ([IntuitionLabs.ai, 2026](https://intuitionlabs.ai/articles/llm-financial-document-analysis)).

| Resource | Type | Focus |
|---|---|---|
| Fin-RATE (2026) | Academic/industry benchmark | LLM accuracy on SEC filing analytics and tracking tasks |
| AIMultiple 38-LLM benchmark (2025) | Industry benchmark | Comparative LLM performance across finance tasks |
| Arcee AI "Ultimate SEC LLM" | Commercial model | Purpose-built model for SEC filing analysis |
| AlphaSense Deep Research (2025) | Commercial product | Research automation over filings and disclosures |
| Brightwave (2025 Series A) | Commercial startup | LLM-based financial research automation |

*Table 2. Recent LLM tools and benchmarks applied to SEC filing analysis, as catalogued by IntuitionLabs.ai (2026).*

Notably, none of these benchmarks, as described in the available source, are scoped specifically to Form 4 insider-transaction classification or to measuring trading alpha generated from LLM-parsed insider filings. They evaluate general filing-comprehension accuracy (e.g., extraction correctness, summarization quality), not downstream trading performance.

### A Methodological Precedent: Redundancy and Confidence Thresholds in LLM Classification

One earlier but methodologically relevant paper is Boehnke, Pontikes, and Bhargava's (2024) University of California, Davis working paper, "Decoding Unstructured Text: Enhancing LLM Classification Accuracy with Redundancy and Confidence." Although published in 2024 and not focused on Form 4 data, it is directly applicable to how a hit-rate study on insider filings would need to be designed. The authors tested LLMs classifying press-release headlines for acquisition-related content and found that applying a strict self-reported confidence threshold increased classification accuracy, but only when combined with redundancy (running the same classification multiple times); redundancy alone, without a confidence filter, increased cost without improving accuracy ([Boehnke et al., 2024](https://d30i16bbj53pdg.cloudfront.net/wp-content/uploads/2024/06/Decoding-Unstructured-Text-Enhancing-LLM-Classification.pdf)). This is the closest analogue in the provided material to an "academic hit-rate study" for LLM classification of financial disclosures, and it establishes a methodological baseline — confidence-thresholded, redundancy-checked classification — that any rigorous future Form 4 + LLM alpha study would need to adopt to produce credible hit-rate numbers.

### Regulatory Backdrop Shaping LLM Use on Filings

The regulatory environment is also tightening around exactly this use case. The SEC's Investor Advisory Committee issued AI disclosure recommendations in 2025, and Norton Rose Fulbright reported in 2025 that the SEC has "heightened enforcement for AI-related disclosures" ([IntuitionLabs.ai, 2026](https://intuitionlabs.ai/articles/llm-financial-document-analysis)). This context is relevant to any commercial product marketing "LLM-classified insider trading signals," since claims about AI-derived investment signals are now under closer regulatory scrutiny for accuracy and disclosure adequacy.

## Synthesis: Have the Two Literatures Actually Converged?

Based on the sources reviewed, my assessment is that **the insider-trading alpha literature and the LLM-classification literature have not yet converged into a single peer-reviewed study that measures a hit rate for LLM-classified Form 4 filings as a trading signal.** What exists instead are two adjacent but currently separate research programs: one (Oenschläger & Möllenhoff, 2025; Ozlen & Batumoglu, 2026) is actively *shrinking* the addressable alpha in post-filing Form 4 data, while the other (Fin-RATE, AIMultiple, Boehnke et al.) is building general-purpose accuracy benchmarks for LLMs reading financial text, without connecting those accuracy scores back to realized trading returns.

This gap is itself an informative finding, not merely an absence of data. If 70–80% of insider-trading alpha is realized before a filing is even public ([Johnsen & Shasharina, 2026](https://tommijohnsen.substack.com/p/most-of-the-insider-trading-alpha)), and mechanical fast-reaction strategies on public filings already show returns that "vanish and even become negative" once realistic position sizes and liquidity constraints are applied ([Oenschläger & Möllenhoff, 2025](https://ideas.repec.org/a/eee/finlet/v72y2025ics1544612324015435.html)), then an LLM's classification speed or accuracy on Form 4 text is optimizing the wrong bottleneck. The bottleneck was never how fast or accurately a human or model can read the filing — real-time aggregation platforms like StockTitan already surface hundreds of Form 4 filings within minutes of submission, listing filer, ticker, exchange, and filing type as they post ([StockTitan, n.d.](https://www.stocktitan.net/sec-filings/form-4.html)) — but the fact that the economically meaningful information predates public disclosure entirely. An LLM that classifies sentiment, urgency, or transaction clusters faster than a human analyst does not close that gap; it merely automates a step that current 2025–2026 research suggests contributes marginally to realized alpha.

## Conclusion and Assessment

The recent, credible evidence points toward three concrete conclusions. First, the classical 4–8% insider-buying alpha figure should be treated as an upper-bound, backtest-era estimate; 2025 intraday evidence shows it compresses sharply — potentially to zero or negative — once realistic trade sizing and liquidity are imposed ([Oenschläger & Möllenhoff, 2025](https://ideas.repec.org/a/eee/finlet/v72y2025ics1544612324015435.html)). Second, a 2026 decomposition analysis indicates the majority of whatever alpha remains is inaccessible to any strategy — human or AI-accelerated — that acts only after the Form 4 is filed ([Johnsen & Shasharina, 2026](https://tommijohnsen.substack.com/p/most-of-the-insider-trading-alpha)). Third, while LLM classification of SEC filings is a genuinely active 2025–2026 research and product area (Fin-RATE, AIMultiple, Arcee AI, AlphaSense, Brightwave), no source reviewed here reports a hit-rate or alpha-generation figure specific to LLM-classified Form 4 data; the closest methodological precedent (Boehnke et al., 2024) is about general press-release classification accuracy, not insider-trading returns. Practitioners and researchers evaluating vendor claims that combine "AI-powered insider trading signals" should treat such claims with skepticism until a study directly measures post-filing, LLM-accelerated trading performance net of the pre-filing alpha decay these 2025–2026 papers document.
