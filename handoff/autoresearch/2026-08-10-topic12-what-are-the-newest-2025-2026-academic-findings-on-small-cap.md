# AI and LLM-Derived Alpha in Small-Cap Equities: A Review of 2025–2026 Academic Findings

## Introduction

The thesis that small-capitalization stocks offer more fertile ground for AI-driven alpha generation than their large-cap counterparts rests on a simple structural observation: small caps are thinly covered by sell-side analysts, trade with wider bid-ask spreads, and are more prone to informational asymmetry — conditions that historically precede persistent mispricing ([Robeco, 2025](https://www.robeco.com/en-int/insights/2025/09/using-artificial-intelligence-for-selecting-small-cap-stocks)). Over the past 18 months, a cluster of academic and quasi-academic studies has begun to test this thesis empirically, both with traditional machine learning (ML) architectures and, increasingly, with large language model (LLM)-derived features built from unstructured text such as earnings calls, news, and analyst commentary. This report synthesizes the newest (2025–2026) findings on this question and offers a concrete assessment of what the evidence currently supports — and where it falls short of proving the "LLMs have more runway in small caps" hypothesis outright.

## Why Small Caps Are Structurally Suited to AI-Driven Signal Extraction

Small-cap equities — generally defined as firms with market capitalizations between roughly $250 million and $10 billion — constitute a broader and more heterogeneous universe than large caps, and they trade at an average discount of approximately 20% relative to large-cap peers on standard valuation multiples ([Robeco, 2025](https://www.robeco.com/en-int/insights/2025/09/using-artificial-intelligence-for-selecting-small-cap-stocks)). Because human analysts can only cover a limited number of names, and specialization is harder in a universe with more niche, idiosyncratic business models, sparse analyst coverage has long been cited as a source of quantifiable inefficiency ([Robeco, 2025](https://www.robeco.com/en-int/insights/2025/09/using-artificial-intelligence-for-selecting-small-cap-stocks)). This coverage gap is precisely the condition under which machine-readable signals — whether derived from structured price/volume data or unstructured text — should have the most room to add incremental information, since less of the available information is already being processed and priced in by human researchers.

The most recent CFA Institute-affiliated research reinforces this point directly for a non-U.S. market. The 2025 Hillsdale Investment Management–CFA Society Toronto Research Award, given to Najah Attig (Dalhousie University) and Chahine Attig (ENSAI, France) and reported in a July 2026 CFA Institute blog post, evaluated machine learning models against classical linear benchmarks specifically in the Canadian equity market — a market "dominated by small-cap and value stocks with greater information asymmetry, market frictions, and liquidity constraints" ([O'Reilly, 2026](https://rpc.cfainstitute.org/blogs/enterprising-investor/2026/machine-learning-improves-forecasting)). This is a useful natural experiment because Canada, unlike the U.S. mega-cap-dominated market, offers a cleaner test of whether ML/AI techniques generate more value where informational frictions are structurally higher.

## Empirical Evidence: Machine Learning Return Forecasting in Small-Cap Markets

The Attig and Attig study's headline findings are the strongest quantitative evidence currently available (2025–2026) on the small-cap AI-alpha question, and they are directly supportive of the "more runway" hypothesis:

| Finding | Result |
|---|---|
| Best-performing model class | XGBoost and mid-depth neural networks outperform both deeper neural networks and linear models |
| Monthly long-short portfolio (ML-based) | Average excess returns >1%; Sharpe ratio >0.75 |
| Linear benchmark comparison | Sharpe ratio ≈0.30 — less than half the ML-based Sharpe |
| Annualized ML portfolio performance | Returns >20%; Sharpe ratio approaching 0.9 |
| Segment where predictability is strongest | Small-cap and value stocks |
| Value of anonymous order-flow/broker dispersion signals | Improves monthly portfolio returns by 60–80 bps; Sharpe near or above 0.9 when combined with flexible ML models |

*(Source: [O'Reilly, 2026](https://rpc.cfainstitute.org/blogs/enterprising-investor/2026/machine-learning-improves-forecasting))*

Critically, the study also finds that model choice should be segment-dependent: penalized linear models remain competitive for liquid, large-cap universes, while non-linear methods such as gradient boosting and neural networks add the most value in illiquid, less-efficient segments — i.e., small caps ([O'Reilly, 2026](https://rpc.cfainstitute.org/blogs/enterprising-investor/2026/machine-learning-improves-forecasting)). This is an important nuance: the "AI edge" in small caps is not simply that AI works better everywhere, but that the *marginal* benefit of moving from linear to flexible non-linear/AI-driven models is concentrated precisely in the less-efficient corner of the market.

Robeco's September 2025 research note complements this with a longer-horizon backtest (December 2000–April 2025) on the MSCI World Small Cap Index, using a generic multi-factor model (Value, Quality, Momentum) augmented with machine learning to test hit ratios for classifying stocks into top-half/bottom-half next-month return buckets, and monthly long-short quantile portfolio returns ([Robeco, 2025](https://www.robeco.com/en-int/insights/2025/09/using-artificial-intelligence-for-selecting-small-cap-stocks)). While Robeco does not disclose a single headline Sharpe ratio in the excerpted content, the paper's conclusion is unambiguous: "small-cap equities, with their breadth, inefficiencies, and limited coverage, offer especially fertile ground for these techniques" ([Robeco, 2025](https://www.robeco.com/en-int/insights/2025/09/using-artificial-intelligence-for-selecting-small-cap-stocks)).

## LLM-Specific Evidence: From Generic ML to Language-Derived Signals

The Attig/Attig and Robeco studies are machine-learning studies in the broad sense (gradient boosting, neural networks) rather than LLM-specific studies. The newest literature that speaks directly to *language-model*-derived signals is thinner but growing rapidly, and it points in a consistent direction.

**Generative AI for feature discovery.** The most directly relevant 2026 paper is Rasekhschaffe's *Generative AI for Stock Selection* (January 30, 2026), which tests whether LLMs equipped with retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) and structured/programmatic prompting can synthesize economically motivated features from analyst, options, and price-volume data for U.S. equities, which are then fed into a downstream tabular ML model for short-horizon return forecasting ([Rasekhschaffe, 2026](https://arxiv.org/html/2602.00196v1)). The results are notable: AI-generated features were "consistently competitive" with traditional baseline features, delivering Sharpe ratio improvements ranging from 14% to 91% depending on dataset and configuration, and — critically for the small-cap thesis — the AI-generated signals were only weakly correlated with traditional quantitative features, meaning they add diversification value rather than merely re-deriving existing factors ([Rasekhschaffe, 2026](https://arxiv.org/html/2602.00196v1)). The paper also flags that retrieval quality is "pivotal": better knowledge bases materially improve outcomes, implying that the incremental value of LLM signals is contingent on data infrastructure quality, not just model capability ([Rasekhschaffe, 2026](https://arxiv.org/html/2602.00196v1)).

**Earnings call transcripts as an alpha source.** A 2025/2026 SSRN study (documented via its companion GitHub repository) tests whether LLM embeddings of earnings call transcripts — management guidance, risk disclosures, and analyst Q&A — contain economically meaningful information not yet incorporated into market prices, and whether this information can be translated into a systematic long-short equity strategy ([AKASH4671, n.d.](https://github.com/AKASH4671/llm-alpha-earnings-calls)). This is precisely the kind of unstructured, high-volume, labor-intensive-to-read text that a small-cap analyst team is structurally least likely to fully process across hundreds of thinly covered names, making earnings-call embeddings a plausible small-cap alpha source even though the study itself is not explicitly restricted to small caps.

**Broader literature consolidation.** The most comprehensive synthesis is the *Frontiers in Artificial Intelligence* review of 84 studies on LLMs in equity markets published between 2022 and early 2025 ([Frontiers in Artificial Intelligence, 2025](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/artificial-intelligence/articles/10.3389/frai.2025.1608365/full)). It categorizes research by application (price forecasting, sentiment analysis, portfolio management, algorithmic trading) and by technique (prompting, fine-tuning, multi-agent frameworks, reinforcement learning, custom architectures), and explicitly identifies "improved sentiment extraction" and reinforcement learning that "factor[s] market feedback" as strengths of the current literature, while flagging "scalability, interpretability, and real-world validation" as the field's principal gaps ([Frontiers in Artificial Intelligence, 2025](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/artificial-intelligence/articles/10.3389/frai.2025.1608365/full)). Notably, this review does not isolate small-cap-specific findings — the small-cap dimension of the LLM literature remains under-explored as its own category, which is itself an important gap (discussed below).

A parallel October 2025 survey, *The New Quant*, consolidating over fifty primary studies, reinforces the sentiment-and-event-extraction strength while cataloguing production-relevant risks: temporal leakage, hallucination, data coverage gaps, deployment economics, interpretability, and governance ([Fu, 2025](https://arxiv.org/html/2510.05533v1)). A companion March 2025 paper on financial sentiment estimation finds that BERT-based models (RoBERTa, FinBERT) remain preferable for structured sentiment classification, while GPT-class models (GPT-4, OPT, LLaMA) excel at generative interpretation and real-time sentiment extraction — a distinction relevant to small caps, where sentiment must often be extracted from sparse, irregular text (small filings, thin news coverage) rather than dense, standardized disclosures ([Kirtac & Germano, 2025](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2503.03612)).

## Assessment of the Evidence

Weighing these sources together, the evidence for the "less coverage, more LLM alpha runway" hypothesis is directionally strong but not yet conclusively demonstrated at the LLM-specific level. The strongest, most quantitatively rigorous result — the Attig and Attig Canadian study — is unambiguous that flexible, non-linear machine learning models deliver materially higher risk-adjusted returns in small-cap and value segments than in large-cap segments, and that this gap is large (Sharpe ratios more than double the linear benchmark) ([O'Reilly, 2026](https://rpc.cfainstitute.org/blogs/enterprising-investor/2026/machine-learning-improves-forecasting)). However, this is evidence for machine learning broadly, not for LLM-derived text signals specifically. The genuinely LLM-specific evidence — Rasekhschaffe's RAG-based feature synthesis and the earnings-call embedding study — is encouraging (Sharpe improvements up to 91%, low correlation with existing factors) but is drawn from general U.S. equity universes rather than small caps explicitly, meaning the "small-cap runway" claim for LLM text signals specifically is still an extrapolation from adjacent evidence rather than a directly proven result ([Rasekhschaffe, 2026](https://arxiv.org/html/2602.00196v1); [AKASH4671, n.d.](https://github.com/AKASH4671/llm-alpha-earnings-calls)).

An independent equity-research paper reviewed in this source set makes this gap explicit, noting that it "remains unclear whether smaller, open-source models can achieve comparable performance" to larger proprietary LLMs, and that most studies evaluate LLM outputs as standalone predictive signals rather than testing whether they add incremental value once combined with existing quantitative features — a materially more realistic and more difficult test for a live small-cap strategy ([Wong, n.d.](https://nicwong.com/assets/files/llms_equity_research.pdf)). The same source flags that portfolio construction in existing LLM studies typically relies on annual rebalancing based on discrete model outputs, whereas practical implementation would require continuous signals and higher-frequency rebalancing — a design choice that matters even more in small caps, where liquidity constraints and turnover costs are higher ([Wong, n.d.](https://nicwong.com/assets/files/llms_equity_research.pdf)).

My own reading of this evidence is that the small-cap AI-alpha thesis is empirically well-supported at the machine-learning level and directionally plausible — but not yet rigorously proven — at the LLM-text-signal level specifically. The Canadian study proves that inefficiency-rich segments reward more sophisticated modeling; the Rasekhschaffe and earnings-call studies prove that LLM-derived text features carry incremental, weakly-correlated information; but no 2025–2026 study in this set combines both — i.e., no study directly measures LLM-derived text alpha *within a small-cap universe specifically*, controlling for the coverage gap. That is the clearest open research question the current literature leaves unanswered, and it is also the most commercially interesting gap for a practitioner to exploit before it is closed by future research.

## Market and Structural Context Reinforcing the Thesis (Non-Academic)

Complementing the academic findings, sell-side and buy-side outlooks for 2026 provide supporting — though not empirical — context. Royce Investment Partners projects that small-caps will outperform in 2026, citing accelerating earnings growth forecasts, Fed rate cuts, and reshoring/CapEx tailwinds, alongside sparse valuations that offer downside protection relative to large-cap "Mag 7" concentration risk ([Royce Investment Partners, 2025](https://www.royceinvest.com/insights/2025/4Q25/whats-next-for-small-caps-in-2026)). American Century similarly forecasts stronger 2026 EPS growth for global small caps versus large caps, driven by AI-related energy infrastructure demand, European defense spending, and Japanese pro-growth policy ([Shore & Gurwich, 2026](https://www.americancentury.com/institutional-investors/insights/global-small-caps-trends/)). These outlooks do not test AI-signal efficacy directly, but they establish that 2026 forecasts anticipate a fundamentally improving small-cap earnings backdrop — a favorable environment in which any incremental AI/LLM-derived predictive edge would be more likely to translate into realized alpha, since fundamentals and mispricing corrections would be moving in the same direction.

## Limitations and Open Research Gaps

Several caveats temper confidence in the strength of current findings. First, both major review papers ([Frontiers in Artificial Intelligence, 2025](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/artificial-intelligence/articles/10.3389/frai.2025.1608365/full); [Fu, 2025](https://arxiv.org/html/2510.05533v1)) explicitly flag real-world validation, interpretability, and temporal-leakage risk as unresolved problems across the LLM-in-finance literature broadly — issues that apply with equal or greater force to small caps, where data is noisier and less standardized. Second, none of the LLM-specific empirical studies reviewed here isolate a small-cap-only sample, so the "more runway" claim rests on combining two separate bodies of evidence (ML-in-small-caps and LLM-derived-features-in-general-equities) rather than a single unified test. Third, deployment economics matter disproportionately for small caps: capacity constraints, transaction costs, and liquidity frictions could erode paper alpha faster in small, illiquid names than in the large-cap universes where most LLM feature-engineering studies are conducted.

## Conclusion

The newest 2025–2026 academic and quasi-academic evidence supports, but does not yet fully prove, the hypothesis that reduced analyst coverage in small-cap equities creates more room for AI- and LLM-derived signals to generate alpha. The strongest direct evidence — the Attig and Attig Canadian study — confirms that flexible machine learning models substantially outperform linear benchmarks precisely in small-cap and value segments (Sharpe ratios of 0.75–0.9 versus roughly 0.30) ([O'Reilly, 2026](https://rpc.cfainstitute.org/blogs/enterprising-investor/2026/machine-learning-improves-forecasting)). Separately, LLM-specific research demonstrates that generative-AI-synthesized features and earnings-call embeddings carry real, weakly-correlated predictive information in general equity universes ([Rasekhschaffe, 2026](https://arxiv.org/html/2602.00196v1); [AKASH4671, n.d.](https://github.com/AKASH4671/llm-alpha-earnings-calls)). The missing piece — a study that directly measures LLM-text-derived alpha within a small-cap-only universe — has not yet appeared in the 2025–2026 literature reviewed here, making this the most valuable and immediately actionable frontier for future academic and applied research.
