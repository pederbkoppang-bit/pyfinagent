# Retrieval-Augmented LLMs for Equity Research: Do Curated Financial Corpora Improve Prediction Accuracy?

## Introduction

Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG) has emerged as the dominant strategy for closing the knowledge gap between static, pre-trained large language models (LLMs) and the fast-moving, evidence-dependent nature of financial analysis. Because equity research requires grounding in verifiable, timely facts — earnings figures, filings, historical price patterns, and analyst commentary — rather than the parametric "memorized" knowledge encoded in a model's weights during pre-training, RAG is theoretically well suited to the domain ([Knollmeyer et al., 2026](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s42979-026-05134-x)). Over the 2025–2026 period, a cluster of publications has moved from general-purpose RAG evaluation toward finance-specific implementations and benchmarks, providing the first direct evidence on whether grounding an LLM on a curated financial corpus produces measurably better predictive performance than an ungrounded, plain chat model. This report synthesizes that evidence, evaluates its strength, and reaches a concrete, evidence-based conclusion.

## The Case for RAG in Equity Research: Evidence of Improved Accuracy

### FinSeer and StockLLM: Domain-Specific Retrieval Outperforms Generic Baselines

The most directly relevant empirical result comes from Xiao et al.'s (2025) FinSrag framework, which pairs a fine-tuned 1-billion-parameter generative backbone (StockLLM) with a purpose-built financial retriever (FinSeer) for stock-movement prediction. The authors report that conventional retrieval methods — whether generic text-embedding retrievers or simple numeric-similarity approaches — "fall short in handling complex financial analysis," because they fail to capture the subtle, high-impact temporal patterns embedded in noisy financial time-series data ([Xiao et al., 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2502.05878)). Critically, the study includes a controlled comparison: RAG-augmented StockLLM against (a) the bare StockLLM operating without retrieval and (b) StockLLM paired with random retrieval. The finding is unambiguous — the full RAG framework "outperforms bare StockLLM and random retrieval, highlighting its effectiveness," and FinSeer specifically "surpasses existing textual retrievers and traditional distance-based retrieval approaches in enhancing the prediction accuracy of StockLLM" ([Xiao et al., 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2502.05878)). This is the closest available analogue in the literature to the exact question posed: a documented, apples-to-apples improvement in prediction accuracy attributable specifically to retrieval augmentation, isolated from other confounds such as model scale or fine-tuning.

Importantly, the authors also constructed new datasets that integrate a broader set of financial indicators to both train FinSeer and provide a more rigorous evaluation testbed — an acknowledgment that generic web-scraped or open-domain corpora are inadequate substitutes for curated financial data when the objective is forecasting accuracy ([Xiao et al., 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2502.05878)).

### AlphaFin and Stock-Chain: RAG-Enhanced Financial Analysis

Predating this window slightly but foundational to the current benchmark landscape, Li et al.'s (2024) AlphaFin dataset combines traditional research datasets, real-time financial data, and hand-written chain-of-thought annotations to train and evaluate a RAG-integrated method called Stock-Chain. The authors report that the AlphaFin datasets have "a positive impact on training LLM for completing financial analysis," and that "extensive experiments are conducted to demonstrate the effectiveness of our framework on financial analysis" when retrieval-augmented generation is incorporated into the stock-chain reasoning process ([Li et al., 2024](https://aclanthology.org/2024.lrec-main.69/)). While the abstract language is less quantitatively specific than the FinSeer paper, it corroborates the broader pattern: benchmark developers building finance-specific RAG systems consistently report performance gains over non-augmented baselines, and these gains motivated a wave of 2025 follow-on benchmarks.

### INVESTORBENCH and FinRAGBench-V: Benchmarking Infrastructure Matures

Two 2025 publications reflect the field's shift from isolated proof-of-concept systems toward standardized, reproducible evaluation infrastructure — a necessary precondition for making credible claims about accuracy gains. INVESTORBENCH, presented at ACL 2025, introduces the first systematic benchmark for evaluating LLM-based agents across financial decision-making tasks, including stock and cryptocurrency trading and structured investment product analysis ([Li et al., 2025](https://aclanthology.org/2025.acl-long.126/)). FinRAGBench-V, an EMNLP 2025 contribution, extends financial RAG evaluation into the multimodal domain, constructing a corpus of 60,780 Chinese and 51,219 English document pages drawn from research reports, financial statements, prospectuses, and financial news, explicitly designed to test "visual citation" — the ability of a RAG system to ground its outputs in traceable source evidence, including charts and tables that text-only retrieval would miss ([FinRAGBench-V, 2025](https://github.com/zhaosuifeng/FinRAGBench-V)). The emergence of both benchmarks signals that the research community judges curated, domain-specific retrieval corpora (as opposed to general web corpora) to be a prerequisite for meaningful evaluation of financial LLM systems — a methodological consensus that indirectly supports the premise that corpus curation matters for output quality, even where these particular papers focus on evaluation infrastructure rather than head-to-head accuracy scores against plain chat models.

## Where the Evidence Is Weaker: Evaluation Gaps and Retrieval Risk

### The Evaluation Problem: RAG-Specific Metrics Remain Immature

A countervailing thread in the 2025–2026 literature is a set of systematic reviews concluding that RAG evaluation methodology itself is underdeveloped, which complicates any confident, generalized claim about accuracy improvement. Knollmeyer et al.'s (2026) systematic literature review, synthesizing 12 studies on RAG evaluation, finds "substantial variation in evaluation practices" and argues that general LLM benchmarks "under-specify failure modes introduced by retrieval augmentation, including context selection quality, multi-step retrieval aggregation, and citation grounding" ([Knollmeyer et al., 2026](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s42979-026-05134-x)). Gan et al.'s (2025) comprehensive survey similarly stresses that "evaluating RAG systems presents unique challenges due to their hybrid architecture," particularly because performance depends on dynamic, non-parametric knowledge sources that are difficult to standardize across studies ([Gan et al., 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2504.14891)). Brown et al.'s (2025) PRISMA-guided review of 128 RAG articles from 2020–2025 catalogues datasets, architectures, and evaluation practices but likewise underscores persistent gaps and inconsistency in how RAG effectiveness is measured across domains ([Brown et al., 2025](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2508.06401)). None of these three reviews is finance-specific, but their conclusions caution against treating any single benchmark's reported gain — including those in FinSeer or AlphaFin — as a universally generalizable effect size, since methodology, dataset composition, and metric choice vary widely and are not yet standardized even within finance-specific RAG research.

### Imperfect Retrieval Can Erase Gains: The Astute RAG Findings

A second, more direct threat to the "grounding always helps" narrative comes from Wang et al.'s (2025) Astute RAG paper. Through controlled analysis, the authors find that "imperfect retrieval augmentation is inevitable, common, and harmful," and identify knowledge conflicts between an LLM's internal parametric knowledge and externally retrieved content as a critical bottleneck ([Wang et al., 2025](https://aclanthology.org/2025.acl-long.1476/)). This finding is domain-agnostic but has direct implications for equity research: a curated corpus does not guarantee accuracy gains if the retrieval mechanism surfaces irrelevant, outdated, or contradictory evidence — a live risk in financial applications given rapidly changing prices, guidance revisions, and analyst disagreement. The paper's proposed remedy (adaptive, source-aware knowledge consolidation) implies that naïve RAG implementations — simply attaching a financial document store to a chat model — are not sufficient to guarantee improvement, and may in some conditions underperform a well-calibrated plain chat model that at least does not introduce conflicting or noisy signals.

### Broader Equity-LLM Literature: Real-World Validation Still Limited

The Frontiers/PMC review of 84 LLM-in-equity-markets studies from 2022 to early 2025 provides the widest-angle view available. It concludes that while LLM-driven approaches show promise across stock forecasting, sentiment analysis, portfolio management, and algorithmic trading, there remain "critical gaps in scalability, interpretability, and real-world validation" ([Frontiers in Artificial Intelligence, 2025](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/artificial-intelligence/articles/10.3389/frai.2025.1608365/full)). This review does not isolate RAG as a variable but its caution about real-world validation applies with particular force to RAG-based equity systems, since almost all reported accuracy gains to date come from offline benchmark evaluation rather than live, out-of-sample trading performance.

## Synthesis: Does Grounding Materially Improve Prediction Accuracy?

Weighing the evidence, a concrete conclusion is warranted rather than a hedge. **Yes — on benchmarked, structured financial NLP and forecasting tasks, grounding an LLM on a curated, domain-specific financial corpus does materially improve accuracy relative to an ungrounded or generically-retrieved baseline, but this improvement is conditional, not automatic.** The FinSeer/StockLLM controlled comparison is the strongest available evidence: it isolates the retrieval variable and finds that domain-specific RAG measurably beats both a no-retrieval baseline and random retrieval ([Xiao et al., 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2502.05878)). AlphaFin/Stock-Chain corroborates this directionally for financial analysis tasks more broadly ([Li et al., 2024](https://aclanthology.org/2024.lrec-main.69/)).

However, three qualifications prevent a stronger, unconditional claim. First, the gains are contingent on retriever quality and corpus curation — generic embedding-based retrieval underperforms purpose-built financial retrievers, and imperfect retrieval can actively harm accuracy through knowledge conflicts ([Wang et al., 2025](https://aclanthology.org/2025.acl-long.1476/)). Second, the evaluation science underpinning these claims is itself immature and inconsistent across studies, meaning reported effect sizes are not yet comparable or fully generalizable across benchmarks ([Knollmeyer et al., 2026](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s42979-026-05134-x); [Gan et al., 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2504.14891)). Third, essentially all documented gains come from offline, benchmark-style evaluation (e.g., INVESTORBENCH, FinRAGBench-V) rather than validated live-market prediction, leaving open the question of whether benchmark accuracy translates into real trading edge ([Li et al., 2025](https://aclanthology.org/2025.acl-long.126/); [Frontiers in Artificial Intelligence, 2025](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/artificial-intelligence/articles/10.3389/frai.2025.1608365/full)).

## Conclusion

The 2025–2026 literature marks a clear inflection point: finance-specific RAG research has moved from general assertions about grounding's benefits to controlled, task-specific demonstrations — most notably FinSeer/StockLLM — that curated financial corpora paired with domain-tuned retrievers outperform both plain, ungrounded chat models and naïvely-retrieved RAG baselines on prediction-oriented tasks. At the same time, parallel evaluation-methodology research reveals that the field lacks standardized metrics and that poorly implemented retrieval can offset or reverse these gains. The practical implication for equity research practitioners is that RAG is not a guaranteed accuracy multiplier by default — the benefit is real but earned specifically through investment in domain-specific retrievers, curated and current financial corpora, and conflict-aware generation strategies, rather than through simply attaching a document store to an off-the-shelf chat model.
